Revenue streams in detail

1. Tap-to-pay commission

Rider pays in the app; the fare is held and released to the operator’s collection wallet after the trip, minus 5–10%. Refunds before the trip are automatic. Core, recurring, scales with trips.

2. Event mobility contracts

Organisers pay per bus per day or per attendee carried (assumption: ₦2.5M–₦3.5M per multi-day event). Includes passes, pick-up planning, live counts and an organiser dashboard.

3. Operator software

Monthly subscription for fleet, driver, booking and payout tools (assumption: ₦25,000–₦35,000 per operator per month). Free tier for small operators to grow supply.

4. Tag Along fee

Flat fee per shared seat (assumption: ₦100–₦120). Hosts keep the cost share; we keep a small fee for matching, verification and payment.

5. Government data licence

Ridership, corridor demand, verified-vehicle register and compliance reports for the FCTA and agencies (assumption: ₦50M pilot, then ₦150M a year). Riders’ personal data is never sold; only aggregates.

6. Later: ads and finance referrals

Sponsored placements once there is audience (no ads run today). Referral fees when operators finance EV or CNG vehicles through partners (assumption: ₦150,000 per vehicle).

Not in the plan: interest on wallet money

Earning on customer balances needs a licence from the Central Bank of Nigeria. Balances stay with our licensed payment partner; we count no float income.

How we reach large revenue

Lever 1: trips

Corridor by corridor. Each new verified operator adds daily seats; each event adds riders. Milestone: [40,000 monthly riders in 18 months].

Lever 2: take rate

Start at 5% to win operators; move to 7–10% as we bring full buses and instant cashless payouts they can’t get elsewhere.

Lever 3: B2B contracts

Event and corporate staff transport are large, prepaid contracts. One national conference can equal months of commuter commission.

Lever 4: government

The verified-vehicle register and ridership data become the city’s system of record: a multi-year licence that is hard to replace.

Lever 5: new cities

Repeat the playbook (events → commuters → operators → government) in Lagos, Port Harcourt and Kano. Each city is a new trip market.

Lever 6: fleet finance

Our payout history shows which operators earn reliably, so we can refer them to EV/CNG financing, a fee per vehicle and a cleaner fleet.

5-minute pitch script (speaker notes)

  1. Cover (0:00–0:15). “Good morning. I’m [name], founder of Tap A Ride. This week, everyone who rode our free buses to the Velodrome has already used what we built.”
  2. Problem (0:15–0:50). “Getting around Abuja is costly, cash-only and unsafe. City bus fares rose almost 40% in a year. ‘One chance’ robberies are common enough that FCT Police have a dedicated unit and tell people to avoid unpainted taxis. And the FCTA has no live data to plan with.”
  3. Solution (0:50–1:20). “Tap A Ride is one app for every shared ride in Abuja: verified buses, verified car-sharing and event rides, all cashless. Riders get safety and fair prices, operators get full buses and guaranteed payment, government gets data.”
  4. Product (1:20–1:40). “It’s live today at taparide.com: rider app, partner dashboards, payments.” Hold up your phone: book, pass, tap to pay.
  5. Traction (1:40–2:15). “We are the official ride partner of this event. Free buses ran every morning from six pick-up points. [N] people signed up, [N] got personal passes and we carried [N] riders.”
  6. Market (2:15–2:40). “Published figures only count private-car hailing: about $303M in Nigeria. Most Abuja trips are buses and shared cars paid in cash, about ₦850 billion a year by our estimate. Two percent of that is a ₦2 billion revenue business.”
  7. Business model (2:40–3:10). “We take 5–10% of each tap-to-pay fare when we release it to the operator after the trip. On top: event contracts, operator software, Tag Along fees and a government data licence.”
  8. Competition (3:10–3:30). “Ride-hailing apps sell private trips most people can’t afford daily. Local buses are cheap but cash-only and unverified. We are shared, verified and cashless, with events and government data built in.”
  9. Go to market (3:30–3:50). “Events are our wedge: one event puts us on thousands of phones and the organiser pays. Ride credit turns them into commuters; commuter demand brings operators; operators bring government data.”
  10. Roadmap and team (3:50–4:20). “Next: Android and iPhone apps with NFC tap-to-pay, our first verified operators, and Hausa, Yoruba and Igbo.” One line per team member.
  11. Ask (4:20–4:45). “We’re raising [amount] to build native apps and tap-to-pay, onboard operators and grow through events. In 18 months: [milestones].”
  12. Vision (4:45–5:00). “Every ride in Nigeria: safe, shared and one tap away. Abuja first. Thank you.”

Q&A preparation

“Why won’t Bolt or Uber do this?”

Their model is private-car trips at higher fares. Shared buses need operator relationships, verification and event logistics on the ground: slow, local work that is our advantage.

“How do you make money if Tech Week was free?”

Tech Week was our launch and proof. Event contracts are paid by organisers; the sign-up ride reward is a capped acquisition cost.

“Is the wallet legal?”

Money moves through Bachs, our licensed payment processor. We don’t hold or lend customer money ourselves and earn no interest on balances.

“How do you keep riders safe?”

Every operator, vehicle and driver is checked before going live (licence, plate, VIN, insurance). Passes carry the rider’s name and are checked with a photo ID. Riders can share trips with family.

“Why will operators join?”

Fuller buses, guaranteed cashless payment after each trip, and free fleet software. No more cash losses.

“What do you need the money for?”

Native apps with NFC tap-to-pay, a field team to onboard operators, and event-led growth. Name the amount and milestones.
