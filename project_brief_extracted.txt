Abuja Smart Mobility Platform Concept & Strategy  ·  Young Innovators Smart Mobility Team  ·  27 September 2026 1. Executive summary We are building the software layer that runs Abuja's transport: one platform where riders book and pay with a tap card or QR code, and operators, fleet owners and government see every trip and every naira. One-line pitch:  Abuja has the terminals and the buses. We are the system that runs them. What it does Riders  see routes, fixed fares and departure times, book a seat, and pay by tapping a travel card or showing a QR code. No cash, no fare arguments, no guessing if a vehicle is safe. Transport operators  (bus companies, shuttles, fleets) each get their own dashboard for routes, fares, schedules and revenue. This is what "multi-tenant" means. Fleet owners and investors  see exactly what each vehicle earns, which ends the driver under-remittance problem. Government (FCTA)  gets ridership data and a verified-vehicle register that supports the fight against "one chance" robberies. Tag Along  lets verified professionals share rides home with colleagues who live in the same area. Why now Abuja Tech and Digital Economy Week (ATDEW) 2026, hosted by Young Innovators of Nigeria, is themed "Energising Smart Mobility: Powering Cities of the Future". Selected National Innovation Challenge innovators get incubation, mentorship, pilot opportunities and commercialisation support. Government is putting new vehicles on the road (electric airport shuttle, 100 electric buses for civil servants, CNG buses nationwide), but the software to run them is missing. Working name:  to be decided by the team. This document uses "the platform". 2. The gap: vehicles and terminals exist, the system to run them does not Abuja's problem is no longer only a shortage of vehicles. It is the missing software layer that connects vehicles, riders and money. 1. Idle infrastructure.  The FCTA built modern terminals at Mabushi, Kugbo and the Federal Secretariat, with air-conditioned lounges and Wi-Fi. Months later, commuters call them inactive monuments with no working buses, routes or schedules ( New National Star, Sep 2026 ). The FCTA has said operations begin once it procures private operators under a public-private partnership ( The Eagle Online ). 2. New fleets with no shared system.  Each operator sells tickets its own way: Air Shuttle NG  (FAAN with Possible EVs, NEV Electric buses) runs airport to Wuse 2 every 30 minutes, ₦10,000, paid by POS or transfer only ( Abuja Guide ). Renewed Hope Mass Transit : 37 of 100 electric buses for civil servants launched in August 2026, with no public route map yet ( WithinNigeria ). Abuja Metro  rail, the TFA 360 buses launched with the FCTA, and hundreds of private taxis and minibuses. 3. The investor trust gap.  Private investors are willing to put vehicles on Abuja's roads, but there is no system to track what those vehicles earn. An owner who buys a ₦10 million car and hires a driver only knows what the driver reports at the end of the day. Under-remittance is common, so trust breaks down and investment stays away. 4. No proof of safety.  The FCTA is fighting one-chance crime by requiring painted, registered vehicles and profiling all commercial drivers ( BusinessDay ). A rider still has no easy way to check, at the roadside, that a vehicle is registered. Getting started Sign up on the web app with a phone number and verify it. Add a payment method (card or bank transfer through our payment partner), or buy a ride pass. Collect a branded travel card at a partner terminal or agent, or print a QR travel card, and link it to the account. Choose a card security mode:  Tap & Go  or  Tap & Alert  (explained in the payments section). A morning commute: Banex Junction to Maitama At 6:55am the rider opens the app and searches "Maitama" (or asks the assistant: "get me to Maitama by 8"). The app shows buses near them, with fares and loading windows: "Operator A, Banex Junction, loading 7:10–7:20, ₦500, 6 seats left". The rider books a seat. The operator sees the booking on its dashboard. At the bus, the rider scans the vehicle's QR code to confirm it is a registered vehicle on the platform. The rider taps their card (or shows their phone QR). The device beeps green: "₦500 paid". The phone gets a receipt. The operator's share settles to its bank account automatically. The fleet owner sees the trip on their dashboard. Going home: Tag Along At 5pm the rider opens Tag Along and sees verified colleagues from nearby offices driving to Lugbe, with their departure time, pickup point and cost share. They request a seat, the driver accepts, and both share the trip live with a contact. The cost share is paid through the app. No cash changes hands. Without a phone A rider who does not want to carry a phone (for example, in areas where phone theft is common) uses the card in Tap & Go mode. A daily spending cap limits the loss if the card is stolen, and the card can be blocked from any device. 6. Payments and fares Every ride is paid by tap or scan, at the fare the operator published, and settled straight to the operator. Our company never holds riders' money. How the money moves The rider taps a card or shows a phone QR at the bus validator. The validator knows the vehicle, route and fare, and sends the tap to our platform. Our platform checks the card, its security mode and spending cap. The payment partner (Paystack or Flutterwave) charges the rider's pass or saved payment method. The payment splits automatically: the operator's share goes to its bank account, and a small platform fee goes to us. The rider's phone gets an instant receipt with a block-card button. How route fares work Each validator is assigned to one vehicle, and each vehicle runs a route whose fare the operator sets in its dashboard. Fixed-fare routes  (for example, Banex Junction to Lugbe, ₦1,000): one tap deducts the full fare. Routes with several stops  (Banex to Maitama is shorter than Banex to Lugbe): the rider taps in and taps out and pays by distance, or picks their stop on the validator screen. One card works on every operator on the platform. Fares change only when the operator updates them, and riders see the new price at once. Card security modes (the rider chooses in the app) Mode How it works Best for Tap & Go Pays instantly on tap, no phone needed. Protected by a daily spending cap and a per-tap limit Riders who leave their phone at home Tap & Alert Pays instantly; the phone gets an alert with a "this wasn't me, block my card" button Riders who carry their phone Tap & Approve The rider approves on the phone before payment Large amounts only, such as long trips or group bookings Approving every tap on the phone would jam boarding at rush hour and fail when network is poor, so Tap & Approve is kept for large amounts. How money is held, legally In Nigeria, only a CBN-licensed Mobile Money Operator (MMO) may hold customer funds or run wallets; the licence needs ₦2 billion in capital ( Tetra Consultants ). Phase 1:  riders buy ride passes or pay per ride with a saved card or transfer, processed by Paystack or Flutterwave. Each operator is a Paystack subaccount, so every payment splits automatically, with a flat fee to our main account ( Paystack split payments ). Phase 2:  a reloadable wallet held by a licensed MMO or bank partner. Riders see it as their wallet; the money legally sits with the partner. Reliability and fraud controls Validators and conductor phones keep working offline and sync payments when they reconnect. Phone QR codes change every few seconds and are cryptographically signed, so screenshots cannot be reused. Every card has a daily cap and can be blocked instantly from the app or by calling support. 7. Hardware plan: start with phones, add devices after a pilot We launch without imported hardware. Tag Along: ride home with verified colleagues Tag Along matches professionals who work near each other and live in the same area, so one car carries three or four people home instead of one. The idea.  Offices cluster in places like Aminu Kano Crescent. Someone at one office and someone at the office next door may both live in Lugbe. The driver posts "leaving Aminu Kano Crescent for Lugbe at 5:30pm, 3 seats". Colleagues nearby see it, tap to join, and share the cost. Legal position: cost-sharing, not profit.  The FCTA treats personal cars carrying paying passengers as illegal commercial transport and has impounded such vehicles in repeated crackdowns ( The Eagle Online ). There is a legal route for private cars: DRTS runs an e-hailing scheme that gives registered drivers a unique plate ( Daily Trust ). So Tag Along is designed as follows: The contribution is capped at a fair share of fuel and running costs. Drivers recover costs, they do not earn profit. Our legal team must confirm this position with DRTS. Drivers who want to earn are guided to register under the DRTS e-hailing scheme and then join the platform as operators. Safety design Only verified professionals: confirmed work email or employer ID, plus phone and ID verification. Organisation and area circles, so riders travel with people from known offices. Women-only rides as an option. Live trip sharing with a trusted contact, and an emergency button (built with Find Me). Ratings for drivers and riders; repeated bad ratings remove access. Why it matters.  One-chance crime thrives on strangers in unverified cars. Tag Along puts people in cars with verified colleagues. That makes it an answer to insecurity, not another unregulated taxi. 9. Revenue transparency for fleet owners We make Abuja transport investable by showing owners exactly what each vehicle earns. The problem.  An investor buys a ₦10 million car and hires a driver. At the end of the day the driver hands over ₦15,000 and says that was the day's takings. The owner cannot check. Under-remittance erodes trust, and investors keep their money out of transport. How the platform fixes it Every fare is recorded.  Card and QR payments split straight to the owner's account. The driver never handles that money. Owner dashboard.  Trips, passengers and revenue per vehicle, per day, per route, on the owner's phone. Reconciliation alerts.  The system compares GPS distance and hours driven with recorded revenue. A car that drove 180 km but logged ₦15,000 is flagged. Driver scorecards.  Honest, consistent drivers build a record they can use to earn better terms or a path to owning a vehicle. Honest limit.  A driver can still take cash off the books. We shrink this by making cashless the better deal for riders (lower fares, pass discounts, verified safety) and by flagging anomalies. Passenger-counting sensors can close the gap further later. Result.  More trustworthy returns bring more private vehicles onto Abuja's roads, which means more supply and fewer stranded commuters. 10.
